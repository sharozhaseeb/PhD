Can per-edit validation of optimization edits be cut, and what can the benchmark check actually see?

Optimizers that edit code for speed validate every candidate with some mix of build, tests and timed benchmarks. Study 1 replays real optimization commits through a full validation of each state, measures where the time goes, and asks whether the project's own benchmarks even execute the edited code. The validation rules contain no learned or LLM component. On one pure-Python project (networkx) the answer is that the benchmark oracle, more than build or test cost, limits what per-edit validation can see.

What this is not

  • Not general. The coverage result is from one project (networkx). Extending it to 4–5 projects is planned (E1).
  • Not human-labelled. The 14 optimizations were labelled by two blind LLM agent raters plus an LLM adjudicator, all one model family. κ = 0.948 is inflated for that reason. No human rater yet (decision A14).
  • Not a clean machine. One laptop under WSL2, whose CPU governor cannot be pinned; 8 of 9 benchmark passes are labelled noisy.
  • Not an optimizer session. Historical commits are weeks apart; no session effect is claimed.
  • Not fully explained. About 2 % of benchmark comparisons flag a change even with identical code. The cause is unresolved.
  • No heavy build. The heavy-build project was planned and not run; we make no claim for heavy builds.

4 · The oracle gap · RQ2 and RQ3

Executed, visible, detectable: three separate gates

An optimization's effect can be missed because no benchmark runs the code, because the code is a tiny share of the benchmark's time, or because the change is inside the noise. On networkx much of the loss is at the first gate.

Three different populations: do not read this as one funnel.

5 · Where the time goes · RQ1 and RQ4

Benchmarks are the largest part, by less once setup is reused

As asv runs the suite, one networkx benchmark pass costs about 8× build+tests on the fastest passes. Most of that sits outside the timing loops. Reusing per-module setup removes most of it, leaving 2.2–2.3×.

Across 9 standard passes

659–2,984 s per pass, median ≈ 1,220 s: ≈ 8× build+tests on the fastest passes, ≈ 14× at the median, up to ≈ 34×. Timing loops stay at 132–171 s. In one evening three passes drifted 2,984 → 1,217 s (2.45×) with loops at 144–155 s; the cause is unknown.

measured8 of 9 passes noisy

Build is a minor lever

networkx build median 1.19 s, about 0.1 % of a validation once benchmarks count. bottleneck (C extension): 4.7–5.4 s, 16–18 % of build+tests but 0.8–0.9 % with benchmarks; benchmarks ≈ 18.5–19× build+tests (544 and 547 s, one state).

6 · How far to trust one verdict

About 2 % of comparisons flag a change that is not there

Running the same code against itself still produced “changed” verdicts on 4 of 168 benchmark comparisons. So a single verdict of up to about 1.4× in either direction is not evidence on its own.

Sources: NUM DirAANonSame/Total/Pct/Lo/Hi (4/168, 2.4 %, 0.9–6.0 %), TwoFlips/TwoFlipPct (11/499, 2.2 %), SwapFlips/SwapTotal/SwapPct (6/340, 1.8 %), DirAAMaxDev (1.38: 1.308 slower, 0.725 faster), DirAAFalsePerComp/Lo/Hi, RepTwoLo/Hi; F: N14, N9, N11; R §Directory A/A, §tier 2 dir swap. #8's on-target 1.12–1.20× was not re-tested in the swap.

7 · What is new, and the close work

We report a rate that prior work filters out or does not measure

Per-commit profiling of benchmark reach exists (Traini et al., TOSEM 2021, Java refactorings). In our recorded searches we found no study reporting what fraction of real performance-improving commits have their changed functions executed by the project's own benchmark suite. We measure it for one project.

WorkWhat it doesHow Study 1 differs
Traini et al. 2021Per-commit JMH benchmark reach for refactoring commits, 31 Java projectsPerformance-improving commits, Python/asv, and we report the uncovered rate
Laaber & Leitner 2018Injected slowdowns in 20 sampled API methods; “covered” means detectedReach of real changes, not sampled methods
Peass (Reichelt 2023)Splits missed regressions into uncovered vs noise, JavaWe reuse the split; we do not claim it
Shahedi et al. 2025Exclude commits with no targeting benchmark; report no rateTheir coverage is a filter; ours is the outcome
Grambow et al. 2021Microbenchmark coverage of application-benchmark functions, fixed versions, GoPer commit, optimization commits
SWE-Perf, GSO, FormulaCode, SWE-fficiencyHarnesses that require a workload speed-up or a covering testBy design they cannot measure the uncovered fraction
Ding et al. 2020Tests that cover a change rarely show its speed-upApplies only to already-covering tests
PerfAgent, SemOptOptimizers that validate every candidateWe measure what that validation costs and what it can see
Quansight 2021 (blog)3.7–12.5 % false positives in asv CI A/A runsContext for our ≈ 2 %; not peer-reviewed

Not claimed

  • That per-commit benchmark-reach profiling is new (Traini 2021).
  • That the uncovered / below-noise / not-selected split is new (Peass; Laaber & Leitner). Our “share” is Amdahl's law made operational.
  • That per-edit test selection in an optimization loop is new (PerfAgent uses testmon).
  • That our accumulated-change trigger is a new mechanism: it is a deterministic variant of risk-mass batching (Sayedsalehi 2026).
  • That a two-sided performance gate is new (SemOpt, PerfAgent).
  • That the review is systematic, or that the coverage rate is general. No Scopus or Web of Science search was run.

8 · E1: the extension plan

From one project to 4–5, so the interval becomes useful

At n = 14 the 95 % interval spans 46 points. E1 targets at least 50–60 true optimizations over at least 4–5 projects, which narrows a 50 % rate to about ±12–14 points. E1 is planned and will be pre-registered before mining; nothing is frozen yet.
