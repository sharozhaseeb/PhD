A probability distribution allocates probability across possibilities. For a discrete set, the probabilities add to one. A sample is one random draw. Ten samples need not reproduce the exact probabilities, but their long-run frequencies approach them.

The vertical bar means given. Probability of a label given an image is different from probability of an image given a label. A prior is our distribution before observing the current evidence. A posterior incorporates that evidence.

Mean describes location. Variance describes squared spread, and standard deviation is its square root. Standard normal noise has mean zero and variance one. Its individual draws are random numbers, not always zero.

An expectation is a probability-weighted average. In training, a minibatch often estimates that average. The natural logarithm, written l n on these notes, is the logarithm we use throughout. Exponentiation reverses it. For probabilities below one, the logarithm is negative, so negative log probability is a positive penalty.

[00:02:53] Foundations / How learning changes a weight
Source: Lecture 2 AE and VAE v2.pdf; PDF page 7.
Handwritten source: l2-01-autoencoder-reconstruction-and-a-chain-rule-update.png.

A neural network is a differentiable computation with adjustable weights. It receives inputs, computes an output, and receives a loss: a number measuring how undesirable that output is under our objective.

A derivative measures local sensitivity. If one weight changes slightly, how does the loss change? With many weights, the collection of derivatives is the gradient. Gradient descent updates each weight by subtracting the learning rate times its derivative. A negative derivative therefore increases the weight during this update.

Backpropagation applies the chain rule. When a weight influences an intermediate code, which influences the output, multiply the sensitivities along that path. When multiple paths reach the same quantity, add their contributions.

A vector is an ordered list of numbers. A dot product multiplies corresponding entries and adds them. Matrix multiplication performs collections of dot products. It is different from elementwise multiplication.

You do not need to remember a whole calculus course. Remember local sensitivity, multiply along paths, add across paths, and subtract the gradient. We will use an actual autoencoder calculation shortly.

Takeaway: Update = old value − learning rate × gradient.

An autoencoder receives an observation and tries to reproduce it. Follow the diagram: input x passes through an encoder, producing a latent code z. A decoder maps that code to a reconstruction, called x hat. Both networks learn from reconstruction error.

[00:14:12] Autoencoders and VAEs / What KL divergence is doing
Source: Lecture 2 AE and VAE v2.pdf; PDF page 36.
Handwritten source: l2-04-discrete-kl-direction-and-weighted-log-ratios.png.

The V A E objective needs a way to encourage encoder distributions to resemble the prior. It uses K L divergence, named after Kullback and Leibler. It is a directional measure of distribution mismatch.

For discrete distributions P and Q, take each probability from P, multiply it by the natural logarithm of P divided by Q, then sum across outcomes. The first distribution supplies the weights. Reversing direction changes both the ratios and the weights. K L is therefore not generally symmetric.

Individual weighted terms can be negative, because a probability ratio can be below one. The complete divergence cannot be negative. If the distributions match, the divergence is zero.

On this page, P has probabilities zero point three six, zero point four eight, and zero point one six. Q is uniform over three possibilities. The forward divergence is approximately zero point zero eight five three. Reversing direction gives approximately zero point zero nine seven four five five.

For the V A E we use the encoder distribution as the first distribution, and the prior as the second.

Takeaway: KL(q || p): q supplies the averaging weights.

[00:15:38] Autoencoders and VAEs / Derive Gaussian KL one expectation at a time
Source: Lecture 2 AE and VAE v2.pdf; PDF page 40.
Handwritten source: l2-05-gaussian-kl-derive-it-then-calculate.png.

For one coordinate, let q be a Gaussian with mean mu and variance v. Let p be standard normal. By definition, K L is the expectation under q of log q minus log p.

Subtract the two Gaussian log densities. Their common constant cancels. What remains is negative one half log v, minus the squared deviation from mu divided by two v, plus z squared divided by two.

Now average under q. The expected squared deviation from its own mean is v. Therefore the middle term becomes negative one half. The expected value of z squared is variance plus mean squared, giving v plus mu squared.

Combine these three pieces. We obtain one half times v plus mu squared minus one minus log v.

For a diagonal Gaussian, add this expression over coordinates. If the network gives log variance ell, replace v with exp ell and log v with ell.

A quick correctness check: put mean zero and variance one into the expression. Everything cancels, giving zero. A negative final K L signals a mistake.

Takeaway: KL = ½ Σ(v + μ² − 1 − ln v).

[00:19:54] GANs and conditional generation / Two networks and two objectives
Source: Lecture GANs Part 1.pdf; PDF page 28.
Handwritten source: l3-01-gan-the-game-and-loss-arithmetic.png.

A G A N, or generative adversarial network, learns using two networks. The generator converts random latent noise into a synthetic observation. The discriminator receives real or synthetic observations and scores how real they appear.

The generator wants its outputs accepted as real. The discriminator wants real examples scored high and generated examples scored low. The discriminator is a learned judge; its confidence is not ground truth.

The original value function averages log discriminator score on real examples, plus log one minus discriminator score on fake examples. The discriminator maximizes that value. The generator minimizes its fake term. For ordinary loss minimization software, negate the discriminator's objective.

The original generator term is called the minimax or saturating loss: log one minus the fake score. A commonly used alternative is the non-saturating loss: negative log fake score. Both encourage higher fake scores, but their gradient behavior differs.

An expectation here averages over real observations or random latent draws. We approximate these averages with batches. Next we will calculate the losses and derive why the alternative gives better early feedback.

Takeaway: D: classify real/fake; G: make fake scores rise.

[00:22:48] GANs and conditional generation / Derive the two generator gradients
Source: Lecture GANs Part 1.pdf; PDF page 38.
Handwritten source: l3-02-gan-gradients-why-non-saturating-loss-helps.png.

This is the central reasoning in Quiz One. Name the fake score q. Let it be sigmoid of a, where a is the discriminator's logit before its final sigmoid. The sigmoid derivative is q times one minus q.

For the saturating loss, log one minus q, first differentiate with respect to q. We get negative one divided by one minus q. Multiply by the sigmoid derivative. The one minus q terms cancel, leaving negative q.

For the non-saturating loss, negative log q, the derivative with respect to q is negative one divided by q. Multiply by q times one minus q. The q terms cancel, leaving negative one plus q, or q minus one.

These are derivatives with respect to the logit a. That detail matters. If you look only at the loss derivative with respect to the probability, you miss the sigmoid's effect.

Both derivatives are negative when the score is between zero and one. Gradient descent therefore pushes the logit upward along a useful parameter path. But their magnitudes are very different near zero.

[00:28:31] GANs and conditional generation / Architecture, collapse and diagnosis
Source: Lecture GANs Part 1.pdf; PDF page 46.
Handwritten source: l3-05-gan-failures-diagnostics-and-latent-arithmetic.png.

A deep convolutional G A N, or D C G A N, provides an image-oriented architecture. The generator projects noise to a small feature map, then upsamples through convolutional stages. The discriminator reduces spatial structure toward a real-or-fake score. Spatial size and channel count are different dimensions, and shared filters avoid a separate weight for every pixel location.

Recognize three training failures. Vanishing gradient means the generator gets weak feedback. Mode collapse means many noise inputs produce too few output types. Oscillation means the two changing players fail to settle.

Match the remedy to the mechanism. Non-saturating loss strengthens early output feedback. Minibatch discrimination lets the discriminator see similarity across generated examples, exposing repetition. Adjusting learning rates and update balance can help coupled dynamics. None guarantees success.

Inspect samples from fixed noise inputs over training, gradients, and diversity; a loss curve alone does not establish quality.

Latent interpolation and vector arithmetic may reveal useful structure. An attribute direction can be formed by subtracting codes, then added to another code. Decode and check the result: arithmetic alone does not guarantee a semantic edit.

Takeaway: Weak gradient ≠ mode collapse; inspect quality and diversity.

[00:30:09] GANs and conditional generation / Conditional GANs judge matching pairs
Source: Lecture GANs Part 2.pdf; PDF page 7.

An unconditional generator asks what plausible observation to produce. A conditional generator also receives a request, called y. It can be a class label, a text representation, or another image.

Supply y to both networks. The generator receives noise and condition. The discriminator receives observation and condition, and must judge whether the pair is realistic and matching. Judging the label alone would not enforce realistic images; judging the image alone would not enforce the request.

The note uses noise of length four and a three-class one-hot vector. Concatenating them gives generator input length seven. If the image representation contains twenty values, concatenating its condition gives discriminator input length twenty-three.

One-hot means one active entry within one categorical field. Several concatenated fields can have several active entries. Concatenated widths add; numbers of possible category combinations multiply.

To test whether a generator uses its condition, hold the noise fixed and change y. The requested attribute should change appropriately. Conditioning improves control, but does not automatically repair adversarial training instability.

[00:34:23] Evaluation / Inception Score: two kinds of certainty
Source: Lecture GANs Part 2.pdf; PDF page 32.
Handwritten source: l4-03-inception-score-entropy-kl-and-a-worked-example.png.

Inception Score borrows a fixed image classifier. For each generated image, the classifier gives a probability vector over its classes. For one image, we want a confident prediction. For example, ninety percent cat and very little probability elsewhere. That means low uncertainty for the individual image. Across the whole generated collection, however, we want different classes to appear. The average prediction should not concentrate entirely on cats.
Entropy measures uncertainty. Its formula is minus the sum of each probability times its natural logarithm. A concentrated vector has low entropy. A uniform vector has high entropy. Inception Score rewards high entropy of the average prediction and low average entropy of individual predictions. That sounds contradictory only until we separate the two levels: certainty about each image, variety across images.
The marginal distribution is simply the column average of the prediction table. Calculate that first. Then compare each image's vector to this common marginal using K L divergence. Finally average those divergences across images and exponentiate once.

Takeaway: Confident images + diverse predicted classes

[00:38:40] Evaluation / What Inception Score can miss
Source: Lecture GANs Part 2.pdf; PDF page 54.
Handwritten source: l4-03-inception-score-entropy-kl-and-a-worked-example.png.

Here is the most useful sanity check. Suppose every generated image has exactly the same prediction vector. The marginal is then identical to every row. Every log ratio is log of one, which is zero. Every K L divergence is zero, and the Inception Score is the exponential of zero: one. This remains true even if the classifier is very confident. So a collection of only identical confident cat predictions does not score highly under this formula.
But the score has another blind spot. A generator can memorize one excellent cat, one excellent dog and one excellent bird, and repeat them in balance. Predictions are confident and the marginal spans classes. The score can be high despite no variation within each class and no novelty. Inception Score also never looks at your real reference images. It measures the relationship between generated images and classifier labels.
The entropy derivation says exactly this: log Inception Score equals entropy of the marginal minus mean conditional entropy. When explaining the score, distinguish class diversity from complete image diversity, and never equate a high score with proof of generalization.

[00:40:08] Evaluation / FID compares two feature clouds
Source: Lecture GANs Part 2.pdf; PDF page 71.
Handwritten source: l4-04-fid-means-covariances-and-a-worked-calculation.png.

Fréchet Inception Distance, usually called F I D, asks a different question. We pass both real and generated images through a fixed feature extractor. Think of each image becoming a point in a feature space. We summarize each point cloud by its mean and covariance. The mean tells us where the cloud is centered. The covariance tells us its spread and how feature coordinates vary together.
The F I D formula has two parts. The first is squared distance between the two mean vectors. The second compares the two covariance matrices. Lower is preferred because we want the generated feature cloud to resemble the real cloud. This is the opposite direction from Inception Score, where higher is preferred.
The lecture gives diagonal covariance matrices. That makes the matrix square root simple. The diagonal entries are variances. Their square roots are standard deviations. Do not confuse those two. [00:43:04] Evaluation / Precision and recall use two directions
Source: Lecture GANs Part 2.pdf; PDF page 90.
Handwritten source: l4-05-generative-precision-recall-quality-vs-coverage.png.

One distance cannot tell us whether a generator is unrealistic or merely too narrow. Generative precision and recall separate these cases using neighborhoods in feature space. Imagine drawing a ball around every real point. Its radius reaches the k-th nearest other real point. Do the same within the generated set, using generated neighbors. Exclude each point itself; otherwise the nearest distance would always be zero.
Precision asks: what fraction of generated points fall inside the union of real balls? Its denominator is the number generated. Recall asks: what fraction of real points fall inside the union of generated balls? Its denominator is the number real. The same membership operation is used in opposite directions.
High precision with low recall means samples tend to be plausible, but the generator misses parts of the real distribution. Low precision with high recall means broad coverage with many implausible outputs. These are geometric distribution metrics, not the confusion-matrix precision and recall from a classifier. A query only needs one covering ball. Do not assume that checking just the nearest center always tests the entire union correctly.

Takeaway: Precision: generated -> real / Recall: real -> generated

[00:46:02] Diffusion / Turn generation into denoising
Source: Lecture Diffusion Models Part 1.pdf; PDF page 4.
Handwritten source: l5-01-forward-diffusion-why-the-square-roots-matter.png.

Diffusion gives us a different way to train a generator. Instead of asking a network to create a complex image in one jump, we gradually corrupt real images with Gaussian noise and train a network to help reverse that corruption. The forward process is fixed. We choose its noise schedule. The reverse process is learned.
Call the clean image x zero. After one corruption step we have x one, then x two, and eventually x capital T, which is approximately pure Gaussian noise under a suitable schedule. During generation we start with random noise and repeatedly move toward a cleaner image. That is why the architecture diagram has arrows in both directions.
The critical distinction is training versus generation. In training, a clean image is available, so we can manufacture a noisy input and know the noise target. During generation the clean image is exactly what we are trying to obtain, so it is unavailable. The learned network predicts noise from the current noisy image and timestep. Once that distinction is clear, the equations become instructions rather than mysterious symbols.

[00:47:24] Diffusion / Why the square roots appear
Source: Lecture Diffusion Models Part 1.pdf; PDF page 20.
Handwritten source: l5-01-forward-diffusion-why-the-square-roots-matter.png.

Focus on a single pixel coordinate. Suppose the previous value and a fresh standard Gaussian noise draw are independent and both have variance one. Make a new value by multiplying the old value by a coefficient a, and adding noise multiplied by a coefficient b. Because scaling a random variable multiplies its variance by the square of the coefficient, the new variance is a squared plus b squared.
We want to control the variance rather than let it explode. Define beta as the variance contributed by fresh noise. Then b squared equals beta, so b is square root of beta. To keep total variance one under our assumption, a squared must equal one minus beta. Therefore a is square root of one minus beta.
That is the forward diffusion rule: attenuated signal plus scaled fresh noise. The square roots are necessary because beta describes variance, not amplitude. If beta is zero, nothing changes. If beta is one, the old signal disappears entirely. One caution: variance one here refers to the marginal assumption over data. Conditional on one fixed image, the noise variance has its own smaller value. Do not mix those two statements.

Takeaway: Var(aX+bE)=a^2 Var(X)+b^2 Var(E)

[00:50:09] Diffusion / Alpha and cumulative alpha
Source: Lecture Diffusion Models Part 1.pdf; PDF page 34.
Handwritten source: l5-02-closed-form-noising-derive-any-timestep.png.

We introduce alpha to shorten the notation. Alpha at timestep t is one minus beta at timestep t. It is the signal variance share for that step. Alpha bar is the product of all alphas from the first step through the current step. The bar means cumulative, not an average.
Why a product? At each step, signal amplitude is multiplied by square root of alpha. After two steps those coefficients multiply. Their product is square root of alpha one times alpha two. After many steps the signal coefficient is square root of alpha bar.
In the handwritten example, beta one is point one nine and beta two is point three six. So alpha one is point eight one, alpha two is point six four, and alpha bar two is point five one eight four. Its square root is point seven two. That is how much the clean signal is scaled after both steps.
Say the distinctions aloud: beta is newly added noise variance, alpha is one-step retained signal share, and alpha bar is cumulative retained signal share. Confusing alpha with alpha bar is one of the easiest ways to get an otherwise sensible numerical wrong.

Takeaway: alpha=1-beta / alpha_bar=product of alphas

[00:51:34] Diffusion / Derive the one-shot forward formula

Let us expand two steps so you can see where the closed form comes from. First write x two as square root of alpha two times x one, plus square root of beta two times the second noise. Then replace x one with its own signal-plus-noise expression. The clean signal coefficient becomes square root of alpha one times alpha two.

[00:58:36] Diffusion / What the U-Net learns
Source: Lecture Diffusion Models Part 2.pdf; PDF page 25.

We can now explain diffusion training without any missing pieces. Sample a clean training image. Sample a random timestep. Sample standard Gaussian noise. [01:01:36] Diffusion / Why noise regression relates to likelihood

For two Gaussians with the same covariance, K L divergence reduces to squared distance between their means, divided by twice the variance in the isotropic case. [01:10:49] Transformers / Query, key and value: three different jobs
Source: Transformer.pdf; PDF page 20.
Handwritten source: l7-02-self-attention-the-lecture-s-robot-calculation.png.

For each token, learned linear projections produce a query, a key and a value. A helpful analogy is a search system. The query describes what the current token is looking for. Keys describe what each token can match. Values contain the information that will be mixed into the output. The analogy is only a guide; all three are learned vectors.
We use row vectors consistently. Multiply the embedding matrix X by W Q to obtain queries, by W K to obtain keys, and by W V to obtain values. For one query, dot it with every key. A dot product multiplies corresponding coordinates and adds them, producing one relevance score per key.
Divide the scores by square root of the key dimension. This controls the scale that otherwise grows with dimension and can make softmax too concentrated. Apply softmax across keys: exponentiate and normalize to get weights summing to one. Finally take a weighted sum of value vectors.
The attention output is a contextual vector. It is not yet a vocabulary probability and not necessarily a selected word. Keep the five operations in order: project, score, scale, normalize, mix values.

[01:15:18] Transformers / Multiple heads and dimensions
Source: Transformer.pdf; PDF page 23.
Handwritten source: l7-03-multi-head-attention-ffn-and-add-norm.png.

A single attention head creates one learned way to compare and combine tokens. Multi-head attention runs several such calculations with different projection matrices. Heads can learn different relationships or representation subspaces. We should not insist that a particular head always means grammar or always means position; those roles are learned.
The head outputs are concatenated, not averaged. Then a learned output projection mixes their combined features. The handwritten shape example uses three tokens, model width five hundred twelve, and eight heads of width sixty-four. Each head's query, key and value matrix is three by sixty-four. Its score matrix is three by three, because every query compares with every key. Its output is three by sixty-four.
Concatenate eight head outputs along the feature dimension and the shape becomes three by five hundred twelve. The output projection preserves the model width. These shape checks are a practical way to catch mistakes before calculating any numbers.
Ask yourself what changes if the number of tokens doubles. The score matrix grows in both dimensions. That is the origin of ordinary full self-attention's quadratic dependence on sequence length for its score matrix. Multiple heads change the feature projections, not the fundamental query-versus-key meaning.

Takeaway: 8 heads x 64 features -> concatenate to 512 features

[01:16:55] Transformers / FFN, residual addition and LayerNorm
Source: Transformer.pdf; PDF page 54.
Handwritten source: l7-03-multi-head-attention-ffn-and-add-norm.png.

Attention mixes information across token positions. A feed-forward network then transforms the features within each token position, using the same feed-forward weights across positions in a block. In the lecture example, feature width expands from five hundred twelve to two thousand forty-eight, passes through a nonlinear activation such as ReLU, then returns to five hundred twelve. The nonlinearity matters: two linear maps without it could be collapsed into one linear map. Returning to the original width lets us add the result to the residual stream.
A residual connection adds the original input to a sublayer's output. The shapes must match. In the lecture's post-normalization convention, we then apply layer normalization to the summed features. layer normalization normalizes across one token's feature coordinates, rather than treating all tokens as one giant vector.
Use the tiny handwritten example. Original features are one, three; the sublayer output is one, one. Their sum is two, four. The mean is three. Subtract the mean to get minus one, one. The feature variance is one, so ignoring the tiny numerical stabilizer, normalized features are minus one, one when scale is one and shift is zero.
This is what Add and Norm contributes: a direct residual path and controlled feature scale. It is not another attention calculation, and it does not average the attention heads.

Takeaway: Attention mixes tokens / FFN transforms features

[01:18:40] Transformers / Position is information too
Source: Transformer.pdf; PDF page 36.
Handwritten source: l7-04-positional-encoding-the-full-d-10-example.png.

Consider the same words in a different order. Their meanings can change, but the original embedding lookups are the same set of vectors. We therefore add a position vector to each token embedding before the attention blocks.
Your lecture uses fixed sinusoidal position encodings. For a pair of feature coordinates, the even coordinate uses sine and the following odd coordinate uses cosine of the same angle. The angle is position divided by ten thousand raised to the power two i over model width. Here i labels the coordinate pair, starting at zero. Use radians, not degrees.
Different pairs use different frequencies. Some change rapidly between neighboring positions; others change gradually over long distances. The position vector has the same width as the token embedding so they can be added coordinate by coordinate.
At position zero, every sine coordinate is zero and every cosine coordinate is one. That is a useful immediate check. The formula is parameter-free, so these fixed encodings do not need to be learned. But being able to compute a position vector for a longer sequence does not guarantee the trained model will perform well at that length.

Takeaway: Pair i: sin(angle), cos(angle) / same denominator

[01:21:43] Transformers / How positions reveal relative offsets
Source: Transformer.pdf; PDF page 43.
Handwritten source: l7-04-positional-encoding-the-full-d-10-example.png.

There is one more positional idea in your slides. Take a single sine-cosine pair for position i and another for position j. Their dot product is sine i times sine j, plus cosine i times cosine j. A trigonometric identity turns this into cosine of i minus j. The offset between positions appears naturally. For positions three and one, this expression is cosine of two radians, approximately minus point four one six one four seven.
This provides a relative-position signal. It does not mean that every learned attention score is only a function of distance. An actual query includes projected content and position, and so does a key. When you multiply content plus position by content plus position, there are four terms: content with content, content with position, position with content, and position with position. Learned projections can mix them.

[01:23:16] Transformers / Mask future tokens before softmax
Source: Transformer.pdf; PDF page 56.
Handwritten source: l7-05-decoder-self-attention-mask-future-tokens.png.

During decoder generation, future target tokens are unavailable. During training we process a shifted target sequence in parallel, so we must explicitly prevent each position from looking ahead. A causal mask allows the current and earlier input positions and assigns negative infinity to future scores before softmax.
Why negative infinity? Its exponential is zero, so the forbidden position receives zero weight and allowed positions are normalized among themselves. In the handwritten example the scores are one, two and three, but only the first two keys are allowed. The masked scores become one, two and negative infinity. The value vectors are one, zero; zero, two; and nine, nine. The future vector contributes nothing, even though it is large. The output becomes point two six eight nine four one, one point four six two one one seven.
Zeroing an attention weight after an ordinary unmasked softmax, without renormalizing, is not the same operation. Also distinguish causal masking from padding masking: padding is excluded because it is artificial filler, while future target positions are excluded because generation cannot know them yet.

[01:27:48] Transformers / Teacher forcing and the vocabulary head
Source: Transformer.pdf; PDF page 60.
Handwritten source: l7-07-transformer-training-teacher-forcing-and-cross-entropy.png.

The decoder produces a feature vector at each target position. A final learned linear layer maps that feature width to vocabulary size. These raw vocabulary scores are logits. Softmax turns them into probabilities that sum to one over the vocabulary.
Training uses teacher forcing. Suppose the target tokens are y one, y two and y three. The decoder inputs are start-of-sequence, y one and y two, while the targets are y one, y two and y three. Each input position predicts the next target. The target sequence is shifted, so the current prediction does not simply receive its own answer as input.
Causal masking is still necessary because future positions are present in the parallel training tensor. Shifting plus masking together make the information available at a training position resemble what generation can use. At inference, previous ground-truth target tokens are unavailable, so the decoder conditions on its own generated prefix.
When evaluating cross-entropy, pick the probability assigned to the true target token, not automatically the largest probability in the row. A confidently wrong argmax should not receive a small training loss. This distinction matters in the lecture's sentence numerical.

[01:30:58] Transformers / Generate one token, append, repeat
Source: Transformer.pdf; PDF page 64.
Handwritten source: l7-08-generation-greedy-decoding-vs-beam-search.png.

Generation begins with a start token. Run the decoder to obtain a next-token distribution, choose a token, append it to the prefix, and repeat until an end token or length limit. In encoder-decoder translation, the source encoder representation can be reused across these steps.
Greedy decoding chooses the highest-probability next token at each step. It is simple, but the locally best next choice can lead to a worse complete sequence. Beam search retains several candidate prefixes. After expanding them, it keeps the best few according to cumulative log probability. Adding log probabilities is equivalent to multiplying ordinary conditional probabilities and is numerically more stable for long sequences.
The beam width controls how many prefixes survive. A wider beam explores more candidates at greater cost. It is still an approximation, because pruned prefixes are not recovered. When comparing different-length sequences, additional length conventions may matter.
This distinction is about the rule used to select outputs from the model's distributions. The vocabulary head itself still provides probabilities. Teacher forcing is a training input strategy; greedy and beam search are inference selection strategies. Keeping those roles separate makes the complete generation pipeline much easier to explain.

Takeaway: Model predicts a distribution / decoding selects a sequence

[01:34:09] Final recall / A complete map of the course
Source: Lecture1 Introduction.pdf; PDF page 39.
Handwritten source: l1-01-introduction-what-a-generative-model-learns.png.

Let us finish by retrieving the main ideas without memorizing disconnected slogans. Generative models learn a distribution so they can produce samples. A plain autoencoder learns reconstruction through a code, while a variational autoencoder adds a probabilistic encoder and prior regularization. A G A N trains a generator against a discriminator. Non-saturating generator loss avoids a specific sigmoid-gradient weakness, but it does not automatically solve mode collapse.
Evaluation asks about realism and coverage. Inception Score compares each generated class prediction with their average. F I D compares real and generated feature moments. Generative precision and recall test support membership in opposite directions.
Diffusion fixes a forward corruption process and learns noise prediction that guides iterative reverse generation. Alpha is one-step retention, alpha bar is cumulative retention. A Transformer represents tokens, uses attention to combine context, adds position information, and predicts next-token distributions.
These are different solutions to the same broad problem: learn useful structure from data and generate plausible new observations. For tomorrow, practice explaining what each training target is, what information is available at inference, and how you would check the output. Those three questions organize almost the entire syllabus.

Takeaway: Distribution -> training signal -> sampling -> evaluation
