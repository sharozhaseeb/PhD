[05:40] The big idea: two chains
Before the code, the big idea, with a diagram. Along the top, from left to right, you see the forward chain. It starts at x zero, the clean image, then x one, x two, and so on, up to x T, which is pure noise. Each arrow adds a little random noise. These forward arrows are called q. They are fixed, and nothing is learned there. Along the bottom, from right to left, you see the reverse chain. It starts from pure noise. At each arrow, the U-Net looks at the noisy image and the step number, and guesses the noise inside. These backward arrows are called p theta, because they are learned. We remove part of the guess, and step by step a new image appears. That is DDPM, the denoising diffusion probabilistic model. Training only needs the top chain. Sampling has to walk the bottom chain, one step at a time.

[06:38] Notation card: hat, bar and theta
Before any formula, a notation card. Keep these three marks in mind for the rest of the video. A hat on a symbol means an estimate. x hat zero is the model's guess of the clean image, which it cannot see. A bar on top means a cumulative product. Alpha bar t is every alpha multiplied together, up to step t. A small theta underneath means learned by gradient descent. Epsilon theta comes out of the U-Net, whose weights are theta. Anything without theta is either fixed, like beta, alpha and alpha bar, or randomly drawn, like epsilon, t and the training image. A subscript t just means at step t. And the wavy sign followed by N of zero and I means drawn from a standard Gaussian. One warning: in Assignment 3, LoRA also has an alpha. That one is a scaling factor, and has nothing to do with the diffusion alpha t.

[07:41] Formula card: β, α and ᾱ
Our first formula card. Whenever a formula appears, we stop and read every symbol. t is the step number, from 0 to 999. Capital T is the total, 1000 steps. Beta t is how much noise variance we add at step t. It is small, between 0.0001 and 0.02. Alpha t is simply one minus beta t: the share of the signal's variance that survives that one step. If beta is 0.02, alpha is 0.98, so 98 percent of the variance survives, and the image itself is scaled by the square root of 0.98, about 0.99. Alpha bar t, with a bar on top, multiplies all the alphas from step zero up to step t. The bar means accumulated. So alpha bar tells you how much of the original signal's variance is left after t steps. Near one, the image is almost clean. Near zero, it is almost pure noise. One indexing detail. The DDPM paper counts steps from 1 to T, but our code counts from 0 to 999. So our alpha bar t is the paper's alpha bar t plus one, and our alpha bar zero already includes one step of noise. It is 0.9999 for linear and 0.99996 for cosine, very close to one, but not exactly one.

[09:18] Formula card: the closed form q(xt | x0)
The most useful formula of Part A, the closed form. q of x t given x zero is the name of the forward process: the distribution of the noisy image, given the clean one. x zero is the clean image. x t is the noisy image at step t. Epsilon is random Gaussian noise, with mean zero and variance one. The formula says: x t equals the square root of alpha bar t times x zero, plus the square root of one minus alpha bar t, times epsilon. Think of a mixing desk with two sliders. The first slider keeps some of the image. The second mixes in some noise. As t grows, the image slider goes down and the noise slider goes up. The square roots make the two squared coefficients add up to exactly one. So if x zero had variance one, x t would too, at every step. That is why it is called variance preserving. Why can we jump straight to any step t? Each forward step adds Gaussian noise, and a chain of Gaussian steps is itself one Gaussian. So the whole chain collapses into this single formula.

[11:59] Formula card: the cosine schedule
A formula card for the cosine schedule. t over T is how far through the process we are, from zero at the start to one at the end. s is a small offset, 0.008, added so the very first steps are not too gentle. Pi over two is a quarter turn, so the cosine squared slides smoothly from one down to zero. f of t is that cosine squared curve. Dividing by f of zero makes the curve start at one, before any noise. Because our steps start at zero, our first alpha bar is f of one over f of zero, which is 0.99996. Then each beta is worked out from neighbouring alpha bars: one minus alpha bar at t plus one, over alpha bar at t. In words, the fraction of signal this one step removes. Finally beta is clamped at 0.999. Why define alpha bar first and derive beta from it? Because alpha bar is the curve we actually care about, the signal left. Cosine designs that curve directly, falling slowly at first and steadily through the middle, and beta is just whatever produces it.

[26:27] Formula card: ε, εθ and the training loss
A formula card for the training loss. Epsilon is the true noise we added. We know it exactly, because we drew it ourselves. Epsilon theta is the network's guess of that noise. The small theta means made by the network with weights theta. Epsilon theta of x t and t says the guess looks at two things: the noisy image, and the step number. The double bars squared mean the squared difference, averaged over all pixels. That is the mean squared error. So the loss asks: how far was the guess from the real noise? The network making the guess is the U-Net. It shrinks the image to see the big picture, then grows it back, so its output has the same size as the noise. Which symbols are random and which are learned? x zero, t and epsilon are all drawn at random. Only theta is learned. Why predict noise, not the clean image? Noise has the same simple scale at every step, which gives a stable target, and Ho and colleagues showed this loss is a simplified form of the proper likelihood bound, called the ELBO.

[42:20] Formula card: one reverse step
A formula card for the reverse step from the handout. x t is the current noisy image, and x t minus one is the slightly cleaner image we want next. [43:23] Formula card: x̂0 and the clamp
One more card, for x hat zero. A hat on a symbol means estimate. So x hat zero is not the real clean image. It is the model's current guess of it. Where does the formula come from? Take the closed form, and solve it backwards for x zero, using the guessed noise instead of the real noise. So x hat zero equals x t minus root of one minus alpha bar t times epsilon theta, all divided by root alpha bar t. [84:02] Diagram: the linear probe
This diagram shows the linear probe. On the left, a photo goes into the frozen backbone, ViT or Swin. Frozen means its weights never change. Out comes one feature vector of 768 numbers. Think of the backbone as an expert describer, writing 768 numbers about each photo. Next, a standard scaler puts every feature on the same scale. Then logistic regression, the simplest classifier, picks one of the four classes on the right. Only that last box is trained. If such a simple classifier separates your classes, the describer already places similar photos close together. So the probe measures the backbone's features, not a clever classifier. It also suits tiny data: 70 training photos are far too few to fine tune a big model, but plenty for this.

[84:59] Formula card: logistic regression
A formula card for the probe. z is the photo's feature vector, 768 numbers, after scaling. The standard scaler subtracts each feature's mean and divides by its standard deviation, both measured on the training photos. W is a weight matrix and b a bias. W times z plus b gives one score per class, so four scores. Softmax turns the four scores into four probabilities that add up to one. p of k given z is the probability of class k for this photo, and the prediction is the class with the highest probability. W and b are the only things trained, about three thousand numbers, against tens of millions in the backbone. Why freeze the backbone? Because we want to measure how linearly separable the pretrained features already are. Why standardise? Logistic regression works best when every feature is on the same scale. And the scaler is fitted on the training photos only, so no test statistics leak into training.

[86:06] Formula card: accuracy, macro-F1 and log-loss
The three metrics. Accuracy is simple: correct predictions over the total. For F1, we first need precision and recall for each class. Precision asks: of the photos we called shoe, how many really are shoes? Recall asks: of the real shoes, how many did we find? F1 combines them: two times precision times recall, over their sum. Macro F1 averages the four per-class F1 scores, so every class counts equally. Log loss looks at confidence. For each test photo, take the probability given to the true class, and take its log. Average over the N photos, here 30, and flip the sign. If the model is sure and right, the probability is near one, its log near zero, so the loss is small. Lower is better.

[98:10] Part C · The attention cost question
The last question: for one attention layer in stage 1, compute two costs and their ratio. Omega of MSA is global attention, every token to every token: four h w C squared, plus two times h w squared times C. [100:11] Formula card: Ω(MSA) and Ω(W-MSA)
Omega means cost, counted in multiply-adds. h and w are the height and width of the token grid in stage 1, so h times w is the number of tokens. C is the channels per token, and M is the window size, 7, so M squared is 49. The first term, four h w C squared, is shared. Attention makes a query, key and value for every token, plus an output projection: four C by C matrices applied to h w tokens. The second term has two parts. Q times K transposed compares every query with every key: h w times h w pairs, each costing C. Then the attention weights times V mixes the values, at the same cost. Together, two h w squared C. In window attention, each token is compared only with its 49 window neighbours: two M squared h w C. Each query now has only 49 keys, a fixed number, so window cost grows in a straight line with h w, while global cost grows with its square. What do plain windows lose? Tokens in different windows never meet. Shifted windows, moved by about half a window in alternate blocks, let neighbouring windows exchange information, at the same cost as plain windows. Like the Swin paper, these counts ignore softmax and heads.
