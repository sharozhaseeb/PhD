[01:21] A3 · The two models, and what fine-tuning means
Every group uses the same two models: XLM-R base, and MuRIL base cased, which was pre-trained on Indian languages, including Urdu. Both are encoders. An encoder reads a whole sentence and turns it into a list of numbers that capture its meaning. On top we add a small classification head, a tiny network that outputs three scores, one per label. The highest score is the prediction. The head starts random, because the pre-trained model has never seen our labels. Fine-tuning means taking the pre-trained model and continuing to train it on our labelled tweets. With our head, XLM-R has 278,045,955 parameters and MuRIL has 237,558,531. The four methods differ only in which of those parameters are allowed to change.

[03:14] Notation card · Symbols used in this video
Before the first formula, a notation card, so no symbol surprises you. A hat on top of a letter means an estimate or a prediction. [04:17] Formula card · Softmax and cross-entropy loss
Every method is trained with the same loss, so let's read it slowly. The head outputs three raw scores called logits, written z c, one for each class c. Softmax turns them into probabilities. For each class we take e to the power z c, then divide by the sum of e to the power z j over all classes j. The results are positive and add up to 1, so p c is the model's probability for class c. The loss, L, is minus the log of the probability given to the true label, y. This is called cross-entropy. If the model is sure and right, p y is 1, and the log of 1 is zero, so the loss is zero. If it guesses one third for every class, the loss is log 3, about 1.10. The prediction, y hat, is simply the class with the highest p c. Training pushes the probability of the right label up, and the loss down.

[13:14] Part 1 · The tokenizer analysis the handout asks for
The second half of Part 1 is the tokenizer analysis, the evidence for RQ2. A model cannot read letters directly. A tokenizer cuts the text into pieces from a fixed vocabulary, and each piece becomes a number. Rare words are split into smaller pieces called subwords. For both models the handout asks for three numbers: the fertility, the share of texts truncated, meaning cut off, at our chosen maximum length, and the unknown-token rate. Then it asks us to choose the maximum length from the 95th percentile of token lengths, and justify it. The next slide gives each of these as a small formula.

[13:57] Formula card · Fertility, UNK rate, truncation and p95
Let's go through these one at a time. Fertility is the number of subword tokens divided by the number of words, where a word is anything between spaces. Special start and end tokens are not counted here. A fertility of 1 means one piece per word. Why does high fertility cost? Longer sequences mean more compute, and attention cost grows with the square of the length. More tweets get cut off, and the model sees fragments instead of whole units of meaning. For XLM-R on our training split, 17,105 tokens divided by 12,208 words gives 1.401. The unknown rate is the number of unknown tokens divided by all subword tokens. An unknown token appears when the tokenizer meets characters it cannot represent at all, so that meaning is lost. The truncation rate is the number of tweets longer than max length, divided by all tweets. And p95 is the length, counting special tokens, that 95 percent of training tweets fit inside. Our two p95 values were 43 and 41, so max length is the larger, 43.

[15:16] Diagram · One sentence, two tokenizers

[23:44] Formula card · Precision, recall, F1 and macro-F1
Let's build macro F1 for one class, say Negative. T P, true positives, are Negative tweets we called Negative. F P, false positives, are other tweets we wrongly called Negative. F N, false negatives, are Negative tweets we missed. Precision, P, is T P divided by T P plus F P: of the tweets we called Negative, how many really were? Recall, R, is T P divided by T P plus F N: of the truly Negative tweets, how many did we find? F1 is two P R over P plus R, which is high only if both are high. Macro F1 is the plain average of the three class F1 scores. So the tiny Objective class, with 3 test tweets, counts as much as Negative with 75. That is why it suits imbalanced data. Now the lazy model that always says Negative. Precision is 75 over 150, 0.5, and recall is 1, so F1 is two thirds. The other classes score zero, and two thirds divided by three is 0.222, even though accuracy is 0.5.

[27:31] Formula card · Peak memory with max_memory_allocated
Now the memory formula. Allocated of t is the number of bytes held by live PyTorch tensors at moment t: weights, gradients, optimizer values and activations. The peak is the maximum of that over the whole training run. Calling reset peak memory stats starts a new maximum, and we call it after the model is loaded, just before training. Memory that PyTorch has reserved but is not using is not counted, so the number is lower than what the GPU monitor shows. To get mebibytes, written M i B, divide bytes by two to the power twenty, which is 1,048,576. For example, full fine-tuning with seed 3 peaked at 5,240,331,776 bytes, which is 4,997.6 mebibytes. The intuition: this number is a lower bound on the GPU memory this training needs, because the CUDA context and the reserved cache come on top.

[32:11] Formula card · Gradient accumulation and the effective batch
The handout says effective batch 16, and allows gradient accumulation. Here is what that means. The microbatch is how many tweets go through the GPU at once; ours is 8. The accumulation steps is how many microbatches we run before updating; ours is 2. Effective batch equals microbatch times accumulation steps, so 8 times 2 is 16. In the formula, g one and g two are the gradients from the two microbatches. They are combined into one average gradient g, and only then do the weights change, once. The result is the same update as a batch of 16, but only 8 tweets sit in memory at a time. With 697 training tweets, that gives 44 updates per epoch, rounding up, and 132 updates over three epochs. At 25 percent of the data, 174 tweets, it is 33 updates.

[37:17] Method 1 · Full fine-tuning: the baseline
Now the four methods, starting with full fine-tuning, which the handout calls the accuracy and cost baseline. Every single weight is allowed to change. That usually gives the best accuracy, but it is also the most expensive, because the optimizer must keep extra values for every trained weight. The other three methods are the parameter-efficient ones from the PEFT library. They freeze most of the model and train only a small part. All four use the Hugging Face Trainer, so the training loop is identical, and only the set of trainable weights changes.

[37:54] Formula card · The weight update
Here is the basic training step. W is a weight. The arrow means: replace W with the new value on the right. Nabla W of L is the gradient, which says how much the loss L would change if we nudged W, and in which direction. Eta is the learning rate. So the update says: move each weight a small step, of size eta, in the direction that lowers the loss. In full fine-tuning this happens to every one of the 278 million XLM-R weights. In practice we use AdamW, which adjusts each weight's step using two running averages of its past gradients. That is the key to memory: for every trained weight, we store the weight, its gradient and two Adam averages, each 4 bytes in 32-bit, so about 16 bytes per trained weight. Optimizer state exists only for trainable weights. PEFT methods apply this same update, but only to their small add-on and the head.

[47:12] Method 2 · LoRA: what the handout asks
Method two is LoRA, short for low-rank adaptation. The handout says one specific thing: use task type SEQ underscore CLS, so that the classification head is also trained. Why does that matter? LoRA freezes the original model. But our head is brand new and random. If it were frozen too, the model could never learn to map its features to our three labels. Setting the task type to sequence classification keeps the head trainable. The rank and alpha settings come later in the experiment table: rank 8 or 16, with alpha fixed at 16, on the query and value matrices. First, the formula, then two diagrams.

[48:01] Formula card · LoRA
Here is LoRA as a formula. x is the input vector, with k numbers, and h is the output, with d numbers. W zero is the frozen pre-trained weight matrix, d by k; inside attention that is 768 by 768. LoRA adds a second path. A is a small r by k matrix that squeezes the k inputs down to just r numbers. B is d by r and expands them back to d outputs. r is the rank, 8 or 16 in our experiments. Alpha is a scale, fixed at 16 by the handout, so alpha over r is 2 for rank 8 and 1 for rank 16. The two paths are added. A starts random and B starts at zero, so at the very first step B A x is zero and the model behaves exactly like the pre-trained one. Why is one of them zero and the other random? One must be zero so that B A x is zero at the start, and the other must be random so that gradients can flow. LoRA's convention is B equals zero; the reverse would also work. Alpha over r is meant to keep the update size steady when you change r; but with alpha held at 16, the scale is 2 at rank 8 and 1 at rank 16. In words: keep the big matrix, and learn a small, cheap correction to it.

[49:27] Diagram · The LoRA side path
Here is the LoRA formula as a picture. On the left, the input x, with k equal 768 numbers, splits into two paths. The bottom path is the big grey box, W zero, 768 by 768, frozen. [57:39] Method 3 · QLoRA: what the handout asks
Method three is QLoRA, meaning quantized LoRA. The handout gives an exact recipe. One: load the base model in 4-bit NF4, using a BitsAndBytesConfig. Two: call prepare model for k bit training. Three: add LoRA, just as before. And because the T4 GPU does not support bfloat 16 well, the compute data type must be float 16. The order matters. Quantize first, prepare second, and add the adapters last, so the adapters themselves are never quantized. Next, what 4-bit NF4 actually means, because that is where the memory saving comes from.

[58:23] Formula card · QLoRA: NF4 storage, fp16 compute, paging
Here is QLoRA in symbols. A normal weight uses 32 bits. In 4 bits there are only 16 possible values, the levels q. Each weight is stored as a 4-bit code that picks one level, and each block of 64 weights shares one scale, s, its largest absolute value. So a weight is approximately s times q of its code. NF4, normal float 4, places the 16 levels at quantiles of a normal distribution, because trained weights are roughly bell-shaped, mostly near zero; the levels are not evenly spaced, and zero is represented exactly. Double quantization re-quantizes the scales s themselves to 8 bits. Only the frozen base linear weights are 4-bit; the adapters, the head, the layer norms and the embeddings are not. Nothing is ever computed in 4 bits. For every multiplication, in every forward and backward pass, the weights are dequantized, meaning decoded to float 16. That saves memory but costs time. The LoRA path, B A x, is added exactly as before, and A and B are never quantized. Finally, paged AdamW, with 32-bit states, keeps Adam's values in memory that moves to the computer's normal RAM only under GPU memory pressure.

[59:51] Diagram · NF4 versus evenly spaced levels
This diagram shows why NF4 is a good choice. Both panels show the same blue bell curve: how weights are distributed after scaling each block to the range minus 1 to 1. Most weights sit near zero; very few are near the edges. On the left, in red, are 16 evenly spaced levels, like a plain 4-bit integer format. Many of them sit out in the tails, where almost no weights live. On the right, in green, are the 16 NF4 levels. They are packed tightly near zero and spread out towards the edges, following the normal distribution, and one level sits exactly at zero. Each weight is rounded to its nearest level, so with NF4 most weights get a smaller rounding error, for exactly the same 4 bits.

[60:41] Diagram · How QLoRA fits together
Here is QLoRA in one picture. On the left, the dotted blue box is W zero, stored in 4-bit NF4 and frozen, about four times smaller than float 16. When a layer runs, the arrow labelled dequantize on the fly turns it into a temporary float 16 copy, used only for this one multiplication. Above, the orange box is the LoRA path, A and B, the trained part. At the plus, the two are added to give h. The left text box lists the three memory tricks: NF4 storage, double quantization of the per-block scales, and the paged optimizer, which moves optimizer state to CPU RAM only if the GPU runs out. The right box says the key thing: compute is never done in 4 bits. Gradients flow through the frozen W zero into A and B only. Notice the label: A and B are kept in full precision. They are never quantized; only the frozen base is. Our result: 943 mebibytes against 4,997 for full fine-tuning, at macro F1 0.363.

[71:25] Method 4 · IA3: what the handout asks
Method four is IA3, short for infused adapter by inhibiting and amplifying inner activations. Instead of adding new matrices, IA3 learns three vectors per layer that rescale the keys, the values and the feed-forward activations, element by element. The vectors start at 1, which changes nothing, so training begins from the pre-trained model. Values above 1 amplify a feature, and below 1 inhibit it. The handout adds that IA3 trains about 0.05 percent of the parameters, and that we should print the model to find the right module names, which our target names function does.

[72:07] Formula card · IA3
Here is IA3 as a formula. K is a token's key vector, 768 numbers, and V is its value vector. h is the feed-forward hidden activation, 3,072 numbers. l k, l v and l f f are the learned vectors, the same lengths, all starting at 1. Starting at 1 is the identity, so step zero is exactly the pre-trained model. There is never a vector on the query; 07a has only key and value, and 07b adds the feed-forward one. The circle with a dot means element-wise product: multiply entry 1 by entry 1, entry 2 by entry 2, and so on. So K prime is the key with every feature rescaled by its own knob. A tiny example: 1.2, 1 and 0.5, element-wise times 2, 4 and 6, gives 2.4, 4 and 3. The first feature was amplified, the second untouched, the third inhibited. Unlike LoRA, IA3 cannot mix features together; it can only turn each one up or down. That is why it has so few parameters, and so little freedom.

[73:26] Diagram · IA3 scaling vectors
Here is IA3. There are three rows. In the top row, the keys K are multiplied, element-wise, by the purple striped vector l k, giving rescaled keys. In the middle row, the values V are multiplied by l v. In the bottom row, the 3,072 wide feed-forward activation is multiplied by l f f; that row exists only in experiment 07b. Each stripe is one number, one knob per channel, and every vector starts at 1. There are no matrices anywhere in this picture: IA3 can only amplify or dampen what the model already computes. That is why it is so small, 611,331 trainable numbers for XLM-R including the head, and also why it was the least able to adapt, scoring about 0.24, the lowest of the four methods.

[127:14] Cell 10 · Saved output: a nan gradient norm
Now the interruption. This is XLM-R, LoRA experiment 04, seed 103, in cell 10's second batch. Step 1 looks normal: loss 1.142, grad norm 5.59. But at step 10, the grad norm is nan, which means not a number. What happened? With float 16 mixed precision, the gradients are computed on a scaled-up loss, so that small values do not round to zero. If that scaling makes a value overflow, the gradient contains infinities or nans. PyTorch's gradient scaler then skips that update and lowers the scale. Training simply continues. You can see it here: step 20 has a finite grad norm of 1.32, and the loss keeps falling. So the training itself was fine. The training loss stayed finite, so our own loss check was satisfied. But the nan grad norm stayed in the Trainer's log history, and the next slide shows what that did when the log was saved.

[130:40] Cell 11 · Code: the wrapper's safe write_json
Next, cell 11 writes a small wrapper script. It is stored as text with textwrap dedent and saved as run recovery dot py; the same file is in our source folder. The wrapper imports the training script as a module called runner, and keeps the original write json function. Then it defines a replacement write json. If the file being written is the trainer log, it goes through every logged entry and every value in it. If a value is a float that is not finite, there are two cases. If it is anything other than grad norm, for example the loss, it raises an error. A non-finite loss would mean real trouble, and we still refuse it. If it is grad norm, the value is replaced by None, which JSON writes as null, its word for missing, and a flag, nonfinite grad norm equals true, is added to that entry. Then it calls the original write json, which still forbids nan. So the evidence stays valid JSON, and the skipped step stays visible.
