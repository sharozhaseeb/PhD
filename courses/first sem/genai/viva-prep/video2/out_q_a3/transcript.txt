[09:17] Q2 · Tokenizer: where the handout asks about it
The second question was: what is a tokenizer? The handout raises it in two places on page 2. First, it names our two models, XLM-R base and MuRIL base cased, and notes that MuRIL was pre-trained on Indian languages, including Urdu and transliterated text. Transliterated means a language written in another script, such as Urdu typed in Roman letters. Second, Part 1 asks for a tokenizer analysis for both models, and this belongs to research question 2: how well do the tokenizers of multilingual models handle Urdu, and does this affect accuracy and cost? The important idea to say early in the viva is this: a tokenizer belongs to its model. XLM-R and MuRIL each come with their own tokenizer and their own vocabulary, and a model only understands the pieces in its own vocabulary. So we can never feed MuRIL the tokens that XLM-R's tokenizer made. The next slides explain what a tokenizer actually does, and why the two vocabularies are different.

[10:27] Idea · What a subword tokenizer does
A neural network cannot read letters. It needs numbers. A tokenizer is the step that turns text into numbers. It has a fixed list of pieces, called the vocabulary, and each piece has an id number. The tokenizer cuts a tweet into pieces from that list, and the model receives the list of ids. Why pieces and not whole words? A vocabulary of every possible word would be enormous and would still miss new words. A vocabulary of single letters would be small, but every tweet would become very long. Subword pieces are the middle path. Common words stay whole, and rarer words are cut into smaller pieces that the vocabulary does know. XLM-R uses a SentencePiece unigram model, with a vocabulary of about two hundred and fifty thousand pieces. It treats the space as part of the text, so a piece that begins a new word carries a small start-of-word marker. MuRIL uses WordPiece, the method from BERT. There, the first piece of a word looks normal, and every piece that continues the same word starts with two hash signs. If no piece fits at all, the tokenizer outputs a special unknown token.

[11:45] Diagram · How a word gets split: a worked intuition
This diagram is an illustration, not our data. The sentence, yeh film bohat achi thi, has five words. Tokenizer A, in green, keeps every word whole, so five pieces. Tokenizer B, in orange, knows fewer of these words and cuts some of them, so it needs eight pieces for the same sentence. Now let's build the same intuition with one Urdu word. Again, this is an illustration of the idea, not the exact split our tokenizers made. Kitaab means book, and kitaaben means books: the same stem with a plural ending. Suppose the vocabulary contains kitaab, and also a piece for the ending. Then kitaaben becomes two pieces: kitaab, plus the ending en. WordPiece would write the second piece with the two hash signs in front, to show it continues the word. If the vocabulary has never seen this stem often enough, the word is cut into even smaller pieces, maybe three or four. Each piece then carries less meaning on its own, and the tweet gets longer. That count of pieces per word is exactly what fertility measures, in question 4.

[13:00] Idea · Why the two models have different vocabularies
Why do the two models split the same tweet differently? Because a vocabulary is not designed by hand. It is learned from text, before pre-training, by finding pieces that appear often. So the vocabulary reflects the text it was learned from. XLM-R is a multilingual model with one vocabulary shared across many languages, so Urdu has to share space with all the others. MuRIL was built for Indian languages, including Urdu and transliterated text, so its pieces are chosen with those languages in mind. On top of that, the two models use different methods, SentencePiece and WordPiece, which choose and apply pieces differently. Different text and different methods give different pieces. You can see the result in our own audit. On the same 697 training tweets, which contain 12,208 words, XLM-R produced 17,105 pieces and MuRIL produced 16,029. One small extra point: MuRIL base cased keeps upper and lower case, but Urdu script has no case, so that detail does not matter for our tweets.

[16:56] Q3 · UNK rate: what the handout asks
The third question was about the unknown token rate. In the tokenizer analysis, after fertility and truncation, the handout asks for the unknown-token rate, for both models. Let's be precise about what that means. As we saw, a tokenizer builds every word from pieces in its vocabulary. Sometimes it meets something it cannot build at all, for example a character that never appeared in the text its vocabulary was learned from. Then it gives up and writes one special token, called the unknown token, often shown as UNK. The model receives that token, but it carries no information about what was actually written there. The unknown token rate is simply the number of unknown tokens divided by the total number of subword tokens. Why does the instructor ask for it? Because fertility alone can hide this problem. A tokenizer could look efficient while quietly throwing away pieces of the text. For Urdu, a language with its own letters and character variants, this is a fair thing to check.

[18:04] Idea · What an unknown token is, and why it matters
Think of the unknown token as a blank stamp. Whatever the tokenizer could not represent, a rare letter, a symbol, a whole word, gets the same stamp with the same id number. So two completely different things, one positive and one negative, look identical to the model once they become unknown. That is the information loss: the model cannot get the meaning back, it can only see that something was there. In WordPiece, which MuRIL uses, when a word cannot be built from the vocabulary, the tokenizer typically replaces that whole word with a single unknown token. So one unknown token can hide an entire word. In SentencePiece, which XLM-R uses, the vocabulary covers a very wide range of characters, so unknowns are rare. One honest limit for the viva: our audit counts the unknown tokens, but it does not record which words or characters produced them. So do not guess which Urdu words became unknown. Say that the count shows information was lost, and that we did not inspect the individual cases.

[23:29] Q4 · Fertility: what the handout asks
The fourth question was: what is fertility? The handout defines it in one line: the average number of subword tokens per word. Think of it as how many pieces the tokenizer needs, on average, to write one word. A fertility of 1 means every word stays whole. A fertility of 2 means every word, on average, is cut into two pieces. Why does the instructor care? Because the number of pieces decides the length of the input, and length costs money. Longer inputs need more compute, and the attention step in a transformer compares every token with every other token, so its cost grows with the square of the length. Longer inputs are also more likely to be cut off at max length. And if words are cut into many small fragments, the model must rebuild their meaning from pieces. This number is part of research question 2, and later in Part 3 the handout asks for one plot of fertility against macro F1 for our two models, which we will look at at the end of this question.

[24:35] Formula card · Fertility, exactly as we computed it
Let's make the definition exact, because the examiner may ask how you computed it. A word is anything between spaces. We count all the words in all 697 cleaned training tweets: 12,208 words. Then we count all the subword pieces the tokenizer makes for the same tweets, without the special start and end tokens, because those are added to every sequence and say nothing about how well Urdu is split. Fertility is total pieces divided by total words. That is a corpus-level ratio. It is not the average of a separate ratio for each tweet, which would give short tweets the same weight as long ones. For XLM-R, 17,105 divided by 12,208 is 1.401. So, on average, an Urdu word becomes about one and four tenths pieces. For MuRIL, 16,029 divided by 12,208 is 1.313. Both numbers are close to 1, which tells you that both tokenizers keep most Urdu words whole or in two pieces. MuRIL's number is a little lower, so on this measure it splits Urdu slightly less.

[27:23] Idea · Why lower fertility is not automatically better
A tempting conclusion is: MuRIL has lower fertility, so MuRIL's tokenizer is better for Urdu. Be careful. Fertility only counts pieces. It does not ask whether the pieces kept the meaning. Look at both numbers side by side. MuRIL has lower fertility, 1.313, but a much higher unknown rate, 2.196 percent. XLM-R has higher fertility, 1.401, but almost no unknown tokens. Here is why that matters. When a tokenizer cannot represent something, it writes one unknown token. A tokenizer that could represent the same text might need several real pieces for it. So unknown tokens can make the count of pieces smaller, and fertility lower, while the information behind them is gone. Fewer pieces is good only when the pieces still carry the text. That is why the handout asks for both numbers, and why a good answer always quotes them together. Fertility tells you about length and cost. The unknown rate tells you about information lost. Neither one alone tells you which model will classify better.

[31:26] Q5 · Token length: what the handout asks
The fifth question was phrased like this: what is the purpose of the token length? The handout gives two instructions on page 2. Choose max length from the 95th percentile of token lengths, and justify it. And report the share of texts truncated at that max length. On page 3, the fixed settings add one more rule: use the same max length in all runs. Let's translate. Max length is the longest input, counted in tokens, that we allow into the model. The 95th percentile is the length that 95 percent of tweets fit inside, so only the longest 5 percent are longer. Truncated means cut off: a tweet longer than max length loses its last tokens. So the handout is asking us to choose a length that keeps almost all the text, without making every input as long as the single longest tweet. And because max length changes cost and accuracy, it must be the same for every method and model, or the comparison would not be fair.

[32:33] Idea · max_length, truncation and padding
Here are the three ideas behind the question. First, max length is the longest input the model receives. Second, truncation: if a tweet is longer, the extra tokens at the end are cut off, and the model never sees them. Third, padding. A GPU works on a batch of tweets at once, as a rectangle of numbers, so shorter tweets are filled up with blank padding tokens until they match the longest one. An attention mask tells the model to ignore those blanks. Now the trade-off. A long max length keeps all the text, but costs more memory and time, because attention compares every token with every other, so its cost grows with the square of the length. A short max length is cheap, but more tweets lose their ending, and an ending can carry the sentiment. The 95th percentile is a sensible middle: 95 percent of training tweets fit completely, and we do not pay for the few unusually long ones. One more detail: the lengths here include the special start and end tokens, because the model really receives them.

[43:31] Result · Macro-F1 against training time
Now the same kind of plot against training time. The picture changes.
