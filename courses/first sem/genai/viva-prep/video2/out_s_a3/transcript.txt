[02:23] Diagram · How a word gets split: a worked intuition
The second question was: what is a tokenizer? A neural network cannot read letters. A tokenizer cuts text into pieces from a fixed vocabulary, and gives the model each piece's id number. This diagram is an illustration, not our data. The same five words become five pieces with tokenizer A, but eight with tokenizer B, because B knows fewer of these words and cuts some of them. Think of one Urdu word. Kitaab means book, and kitaaben means books. If the vocabulary has kitaab and a piece for the ending, kitaaben becomes two pieces. If not, it is cut into even smaller pieces, and the tweet gets longer.

[03:07] Q: What does the tokenizer do? · model answer
Here is a model answer. A tokenizer turns text into a list of numbers the model can read. It has a fixed vocabulary of subword pieces. XLM-R uses a SentencePiece unigram model with about two hundred and fifty thousand pieces, and MuRIL uses WordPiece, where a piece that continues a word starts with two hash signs. In our audit, the same 12,208 training words became 17,105 pieces for XLM-R and 16,029 for MuRIL. A likely follow-up: could you use XLM-R's tokenizer with MuRIL? No. Each model learned embeddings only for its own ids, so a different tokenizer would give it meaningless input.

[03:57] Colab output · Cell 3: the unknown-token numbers
The third question was about the unknown token rate. When a tokenizer cannot build part of the text from its vocabulary, it writes one special unknown token. Every unknown piece gets the same id, so whatever was written there is lost. Here is the saved tokenizer audit from cell 3. Find the field unknown tokens. For XLM-R it is 1, out of 17,105 pieces, about 0.006 percent. For MuRIL it is 352, out of 16,029 pieces, about 2.2 percent. So about one MuRIL token in every 46 is a blank stamp. The rate is unknown tokens divided by all subword tokens, on the cleaned training tweets, without special tokens.

[05:50] Result · Fertility against macro-F1 (RQ2)
The fourth question was: what is fertility? It is the average number of subword tokens per word. We computed it as total pieces divided by total whitespace words, over all 697 training tweets. For XLM-R, 17,105 divided by 12,208 is 1.401. For MuRIL, 16,029 divided by 12,208 is 1.313. This is the required plot for research question 2: fertility on the x axis, test macro F1 on the y axis. MuRIL, at 1.313, scores 0.222 for every method. XLM-R, at 1.401, ranges from 0.242 for IA3 up to 0.419 for full fine-tuning. So lower fertility did worse here, not better.

[06:53] Q: What is fertility? · model answer
Here is a model answer. Fertility is the number of subword tokens per word. XLM-R has 1.401 and MuRIL 1.313. Higher fertility means longer inputs, so more compute, since attention grows with the square of the length, and more tweets cut off at max length. But lower is not automatically better. MuRIL has lower fertility, yet a much higher unknown rate. A word it cannot build becomes one unknown token, which counts as one piece, so part of its short count is lost information. A likely follow-up: what does this say about RQ2? MuRIL scored only 0.222 while XLM-R reached 0.419. With two models, that is an association, not a cause.

[07:48] Q5 · Code: the p95 and the choice of 43
The fifth question was: what is the purpose of the token length? Max length is the longest input, in tokens, the model receives. Longer tweets are truncated: their last tokens are cut off. The handout says to choose it from the 95th percentile of token lengths. Line 115 sorts the 697 training lengths, including special tokens, and takes the one at position 663. That is 43 for XLM-R and 41 for MuRIL. Line 117 takes the larger, capped at the model limit of 512, so 43. Lines 119 to 121 count the tweets longer than 43. That is the truncation rate.

[08:38] Q: What is the token length for? · model answer
Here is a model answer. Max length is the longest input the model receives, and anything longer is truncated. It is a trade-off. A longer limit keeps more text but costs more memory and time, since attention grows with the square of the length. Following the handout, we used the 95th percentile of token lengths on the training tweets only: 43 for XLM-R and 41 for MuRIL. We took the larger, 43, for every run, so the comparison is fair. At 43, XLM-R truncates 4.30 percent of training tweets and MuRIL 3.01 percent, and batches are padded dynamically. A likely follow-up: why not 41? It would cut more XLM-R tweets, while 43 keeps 95 percent for both models.
