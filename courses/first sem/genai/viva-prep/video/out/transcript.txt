[02:41] A1 · Forward process and the two schedules
A1 is the forward process. Beta t is the variance of noise added at step t, alpha t is one minus beta t, and alpha bar t is the running product of the alphas. The linear schedule spaces beta from ten to the minus four up to 0.02. The cosine schedule from Nichol and Dhariwal defines alpha bar directly as a squared cosine, normalised by its value at zero, with a small offset s of 0.008 so the first steps are not too tiny, and betas clipped at 0.999 so the last step never has beta equal to one. The key equation is the closed form: x t equals square root of alpha bar t times x zero, plus square root of one minus alpha bar t times noise. That lets us jump straight to any timestep. On the plot, the linear curve falls much faster: alpha bar drops below one half at step 259 for linear but only at step 496 for cosine, and below 0.01 at 673 versus 935.

[04:17] A1 · Which schedule destroys the image faster, and why it matters
The two-by-six grid takes one training image, a sandal, and noises it at steps 0, 100, 250, 500, 750 and 999 under each schedule, using the same noise for both rows so the only difference is the schedule. Under linear, the sandal is almost gone by step 500, where alpha bar is about 0.08. Under cosine, it is still clear at 500, where alpha bar is about 0.49. Why does this matter for training? Because training picks t uniformly from 0 to 999. With the linear schedule roughly the last third of timesteps show the network inputs that are practically pure noise, where there is little to learn. The cosine schedule destroys information more evenly, so more of the training budget lands on partly noisy images where the network learns useful denoising. That matters most for small 32 by 32 images. That is exactly your four-sentence A1 answer.

[05:50] A2 · Training with the ε-prediction loss
A2 trains two networks with identical settings, one per schedule. The U-Net is built from a configuration, so it starts with random weights, no from pretrained. Channels are 32, 64 and 128, and self-attention is only at the eight by eight resolution, which made training about forty percent faster. The training step has five lines you must be able to explain: pick a random timestep per image, sample noise, build x t with the closed form, predict the noise with the U-Net, and minimise the mean squared error between true and predicted noise. Both models used AdamW, learning rate five times ten to the minus four, batch 128, float 32, 28 epochs, which is 3,920 steps, about 16 minutes each on the T4. Why 5e-4 instead of the usual 2e-4? Because we only train for 15 minutes, so a slightly larger step learns faster. Why float 32? Because float 16 gave NaN losses in testing, so we kept it simple. One trap: the cosine loss curve is higher, but that does not mean it is worse. With the linear schedule many timesteps are almost pure noise, where predicting epsilon is easy, so its average loss is naturally lower.

[07:44] A3 · DDPM sampling, and why x̂0 is clipped
A3 is your own sampler, all 1000 reverse steps, with sigma squared equal to beta and no noise added at the last step. Here is the one unusual thing you must explain confidently. At every step we compute the predicted clean image x hat zero, clamp it to minus one to one, convert it back into the matching noise, and then use exactly the handout's update formula. This is what Ho et al.'s original DDPM code does with clip denoised. Why was it needed? The cosine schedule's last beta is 0.999, so one over root alpha is about 31.6. Any small error in the predicted noise at step 999 is multiplied about 32 times, x 998 already has standard deviation about 1.6 instead of 1, and the error grows until the samples come out black. Without clipping, 62 percent of the cosine pixels were stuck at black. The linear model barely changes either way. The ablation cell in the notebook shows both, and the code keeps a clip x0 False option that runs the literal formula. Sampling 16 images took about 16 seconds.

[09:26] A3 · Linear vs cosine samples, trajectories, 1000 vs 1
Which looked better? For sandal, bag and trouser, the linear model: its grid has a good mix of all three classes, with sharp sandal straps and heels. The cosine grid is dominated by large over-bright bag shapes, with only two sandals and two trousers. Does that agree with A1? Only partly. The trajectories confirm A1's point, because the cosine predicted clean image already shows an outline at step 900, while the linear one is still noise there. In your run the linear x hat zero even looks like a trouser at step 600 before turning into a bag at 450, which shows the model changing its mind. But a better spread of noise levels during training did not become better samples here: sandals are thin straps, details decided at the last low-noise steps, and the cosine model seems to commit to a big bright rectangle early. Be honest that sixteen samples is a visual judgement, not a measurement. Finally, why 1000 evaluations for sampling but one per training step? Training uses the closed form to jump to a random t, and the loss at that t is one unbiased term of the objective. Sampling starts from pure noise, and each step depends on the previous network output, so the chain is sequential.

[11:14] Part B · Your dataset and the preprocessing rules
Part B uses your own photos: four classes, glasses case, perfume, shoe and switch board, 25 each. Be ready to be asked about this. 92 are real phone photos. Two accidental blurry shots were removed, and the short classes were topped up with 8 augmented copies: a flip, a small rotation, a crop and a brightness change. The report says this openly. The important design point is that augmented copies only ever go into the training split, because if a flipped copy were in training and its original in test, the probe would be tested on a photo it had already seen. Every photo is opened with exif transpose, because phones store the rotation in metadata, and without it images arrive sideways. We never resize by hand. Each model gets its own timm transform, which resizes the shorter side to 248, centre-crops 224 and normalises. Note that the ViT weights use mean and standard deviation of 0.5, while Swin uses ImageNet statistics. That is why each model must have its own transform.

[12:53] B1 · Trace the shapes with forward hooks
B1 traces shapes with forward hooks. A forward hook is a small function PyTorch calls right after a module's forward pass, so it can record the output shape without changing the model, and we remove the hooks afterwards. For ViT base patch 16: 224 divided by 16 is 14, so there are 14 times 14, which is 196 patch tokens of width 768 after the patch embedding. Then the CLS token is added, giving 197, which is why we also hooked pos drop. After that, every block keeps 197 by 768. There is no downsampling. For Swin tiny: the patch embedding uses 4 by 4 patches, giving 56 by 56 tokens of width 96. Then the stages give 56, 28, 14 and 7 with channels 96, 192, 384 and 768. In timm, patch merging sits at the start of stages 2, 3 and 4. It concatenates each 2 by 2 group of neighbours into 4C channels and a linear layer maps it to 2C. Parameters: ViT 86.6 million, Swin 28.3 million. And the FPN answer: an FPN needs a pyramid of feature maps at strides 4, 8, 16 and 32, which Swin's four stages provide directly, while ViT has a single 14 by 14 map at stride 16 in every block.

[14:54] B2 · Linear probe on frozen features
B2 is a linear probe. The backbones are frozen, in eval mode and under no grad. ViT gives its CLS token, 768 numbers, because it was trained to classify from that token. Swin gives global average pooling of its final 7 by 7 by 768 map, because that is how its own head pools. Then one stratified 70 30 split, the same split for both, with all 30 test photos real. A standard scaler and logistic regression are trained on each feature set. Both probes got all 30 test photos right, so accuracy and macro F1 are both one. To compare them anyway we looked at confidence: the test log loss was 0.011 for ViT and 0.017 for Swin. That gap is tiny, so the honest answer is that they are effectively equally good on this data. Why so easy? The four objects look very different, and both models carry strong ImageNet features. If asked what you would change: harder classes, more test photos, or more seeds, since one error already costs 3.3 percent.

[16:35] Part C · Swin by hand (d = 5: 448×448, C = 128)
Part C, for d equals 5. Patch size 4 on a 448 image gives 112, so the linear embedding is 112 by 112 by 128, and stage 1 keeps that. Each later stage halves height and width and doubles channels: 56 by 56 by 256, 28 by 28 by 512, and 14 by 14 by 1024. Windows are 7 by 7, so the counts are 112 over 7 squared, 256; then 64, 16 and 4. For the cost: h w is 112 squared, 12,544. The projection term 4 h w C squared is 822 million and is the same in both formulas. Global attention adds 2 h w squared C, about 40.3 billion, total about 41.1 billion. Window attention adds 2 M squared h w C, about 157 million, total about 979 million. The ratio is about 42. A neat check: divide both by 2 h w C and the ratio becomes 2C plus h w over 2C plus M squared, 12,800 over 305. If you compare only the attention terms you get h w over M squared, 256, which is exactly the number of stage-1 windows; the shared projection term is why the full ratio is smaller.

[20:16] A3 · Part 1: data cleaning, splits and tokenizers
Part 1. Cleaning removed empty rows, URLs and user mentions, mapped Arabic yeh and kaf to their Urdu forms, and normalised whitespace. But MuRIL's unknown-token rate is 2.2 percent against almost zero for XLM-R. [22:03] A3 · Part 2: the four methods in code
Part 2 is the implementation with the Hugging Face Trainer and PEFT. Full fine-tuning updates every weight: 278 million for XLM-R and 237.6 million for MuRIL. LoRA freezes the weight W and learns a low-rank update, W plus alpha over r times B times A, where A and B have rank r and B starts at zero so training starts exactly from the pretrained model. task type sequence classification, together with modules to save classifier, makes sure the classification head also trains. QLoRA loads the frozen base in 4-bit NF4, normal float 4, with double quantisation, which also quantises the quantisation constants. It calls prepare model for k-bit training, and then adds the same rank-16 LoRA. Compute is float 16 because the T4 does not support bfloat16 well. IA3 adds no matrices at all: it learns vectors that multiply the keys, the values and, in the feed-forward variant, the intermediate feed-forward activations, element by element. They start at one, so again training starts from the pretrained model. Values above one amplify a feature and values below one inhibit it.

[23:50] A3 · How each run is measured, and the parameter counts
How was each run measured? Each run uses a microbatch of 8 with 2 gradient accumulation steps, so the effective batch is 16, in float 16, for 3 epochs, which is 132 optimizer steps on 697 examples. Before training we reset the peak memory statistics, and after training we read torch cuda max memory allocated, which is what the handout asks for. The main metric is macro F1, the unweighted mean of per-class F1, because the classes are imbalanced. Now be ready to derive a trainable parameter count, because examiners love this. For XLM-R LoRA rank 8 on query and value: 12 layers times 2 modules is 24, times 8 times 768 plus 768, gives 294,912 adapter weights. Add the RoBERTa classification head, 592,899, which has an extra dense layer, and you get 887,811, exactly the reported number. For IA3 attention-only: 12 times 2 times 768, 18,432 vectors, plus the same head gives 611,331. So for XLM-R, the head dominates the IA3 count, which is why it is above the paper's 0.05 percent. MuRIL's BERT head is a single 768 by 3 layer, 2,307 weights, giving 20,739 for IA3. Finally, the best configuration of each method for the data-size experiment was chosen by validation macro F1, never by test.

[26:05] A3 · Main results (XLM-R) and the MuRIL collapse
Now the results, starting with XLM-R at 100 percent of the data. Full fine-tuning is best at 0.419 macro F1, plus or minus 0.080, using about 5 gigabytes and 20.7 seconds. LoRA rank 8 gave 0.328, rank 16 gave 0.294, and rank 16 with the feed-forward layers 0.354. QLoRA gave 0.363 with almost no spread between seeds, using only 943 megabytes, but it was the slowest at about 24 seconds. IA3 was weakest, about 0.24. And MuRIL: every one of its full-data runs scored 0.222 macro F1 with exactly 50 percent accuracy. You must be able to derive this. The model predicted Negative for all 150 test tweets. 75 of them really are Negative, so Negative precision is 0.5 and recall is 1, which gives an F1 of two thirds. Positive and Objective get zero. The macro average is two thirds divided by 3, which is 0.222. So MuRIL collapsed to the majority class under these settings: three epochs on 697 examples at these learning rates was not enough for it to move off the majority answer. Say that this is an observation, and that its cause, for example the higher unknown-token rate or simply too little training, is a hypothesis you did not test.

[28:08] A3 · RQ1: rank, placement, memory and time
RQ1. Is rank 16 worth its cost over rank 8? Here, no: rank 16 scored 0.294 against 0.328. A subtle point: alpha was fixed at 16 by the handout, so alpha over r, the effective scaling of the update, halves from 2 to 1 when the rank doubles. Does adding the feed-forward layers help? The mean rose to 0.354, but with a standard deviation of 0.145 over only two seeds, so it is not conclusive. How much memory does QLoRA save? Against full fine-tuning, 943 megabytes instead of about 5,000, an 81 percent saving, at the cost of 0.056 macro F1 and 18 percent more time, because the 4-bit weights must be de-quantised on every forward pass. Against ordinary LoRA rank 16, though, it saves only 442 megabytes, because LoRA already removes most optimizer and gradient memory; quantisation only shrinks the frozen base weights. Paged and unpaged AdamW gave identical F1, because paging only changes where the optimizer state is stored, not the update itself. On the memory Pareto plot, among the XLM-R configurations the nondominated points are QLoRA, the cheapest, and full fine-tuning, the most accurate; LoRA and IA3 are dominated by QLoRA, which is both cheaper and better. Across all runs, MuRIL's QLoRA at 778 megabytes is the very cheapest point, but it is a collapsed model, so it is cheap and useless.

[30:18] A3 · RQ3: which method degrades least with little data?
RQ3, data scarcity. For each method we took the configuration with the best validation macro F1, then trained it on 25 and 50 percent nested subsets. On XLM-R, full fine-tuning was the highest at every size: 0.368 at 25 percent, 0.393 at 50 and 0.419 at 100. IA3 had the smallest drop relative to its own full-data result, only 0.026, but it was low everywhere, and it was not even monotonic: at 50 percent it scored 0.322 with a standard deviation of 0.149, higher than at 100 percent. So be careful with the word robust; it mostly shows how noisy two seeds are. LoRA and QLoRA fell to around 0.22 to 0.23 at 25 percent. So the honest answer is: full fine-tuning gave the best absolute scores with little data, IA3 changed the least in relative terms, and MuRIL collapsed at almost every size so it cannot be ranked. Always add the caveat: two seeds, 150 test tweets, and only 3 Objective examples, so differences of a few hundredths are within noise.

[32:04] A3 · Caveats, the recovery fix and RQ2
Finally the caveats an examiner will probe. The Objective class has only 14 training and 3 test examples, and no run ever predicted it, so its F1 is zero everywhere. That caps macro F1 at about two thirds even for a perfect Positive and Negative classifier. Second, the recovery, visible in notebook cells 10 and 11. The first QLoRA runs used the bitsandbytes AdamW optimizer's own default learning rate by mistake. A small recovery script passes 2e-4 explicitly, the affected runs were redone, and it logs three non-finite gradient norm diagnostics as null instead of crashing. Those runs ended with finite losses and weights, but say that this is a reason for caution. For RQ2: MuRIL has lower fertility, so shorter sequences, but a much higher unknown-token rate, and it collapsed. So fertility alone did not predict accuracy here, and with only two models you have two points on the fertility plot, which shows an association, not a cause. If asked what you would do next: class weighting or more epochs, chosen on validation, more seeds, and inspecting the unknown-token examples.

[33:58] Rapid-fire: answers you should say in one breath
Let's finish with a rapid-fire round. Why predict the noise instead of x zero? It is Ho et al.'s simplified training objective, a reweighted variational bound, and noise is a stable unit-variance target. Why can we jump to any t? Because Gaussian steps compose, so x t given x zero is a Gaussian with mean root alpha bar x zero and variance one minus alpha bar. Why no noise at the last step? Because the output should be the mean estimate of the clean image. Why 197 tokens in ViT? 196 patches plus the CLS token. Why is Swin's attention linear in image size? Because each token only attends inside its 7 by 7 window, so the cost is M squared times h w instead of h w squared. LoRA: a frozen weight plus a low-rank product B A, with B starting at zero. QLoRA: the same, but on a 4-bit NF4 base with float 16 compute. IA3: learned vectors that rescale keys, values and feed-forward activations. Macro F1: the unweighted mean of per-class F1, which is why one ignored class pulls it down. And always: choose on validation, report on test, mean plus or minus standard deviation over the two seeds.
